# Helios Outpost · Teacher guide

Helios Outpost is a simulator in which students run an outpost on the Moon or Mars. There is no single right answer: every decision (what cargo to bring, where to put the modules, when to take cover, whether to go out on foot or by rover) has consequences the simulation works out with real figures. This guide suggests how to use it in class. *(Versión en español: [GUIA-DOCENTE.md](GUIA-DOCENTE.md).)*

## Which ages

**Settings → Level** offers three levels. They change how many breakdowns happen and which questions appear at the end of a mission.

| Level | Suggested age | Breakdowns | Questions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Junior | 8–11 | Half | Concepts: gravity without air, shelter, suit range, polar shadows |
| Cadet | 12–14 | Normal | Adds: ice in cold traps, water recycling, energy, mass and weight |
| Expert | 15 and over | 35 % more | Adds: working with v²/(2·μ·g), projectile range, the sievert |

## What students learn

| Idea | Where it appears in the game |
| --- | --- |
| Gravity accelerates all masses equally; air is what separates the feather from the hammer | Key **G** on EVA: the game times the fall and compares it with Earth |
| Mass versus weight | The rover stops six times worse on the Moon: it weighs less, but its mass is the same |
| Projectile range is inversely proportional to g | Key **R**: throw a rock and compare its range with Earth's |
| Power (kW) versus energy (kWh) | Power panel, batteries through the lunar night |
| Radiation is stopped by mass (g/cm²), not thickness | Shelter, regolith cover, solar storms |
| Limited resources and recycling | Water, oxygen and food; life support recycles 95 % of the water |
| Conditions depend on the exact place | The polar sunlight map; real terrain at Shackleton, the connecting ridge, Malapert, Nobile and Jezero |
| Consumables versus range | The expedition 1 km away cannot be done on foot; the rover makes it possible |
| The speed of light is finite | Radio delay to Earth; asking Mission Control for help in an emergency |
| Latitude decides the light | The four real lunar sites: the Sun hardly sets next to the pole and sets for two weeks 120 km away |
| The body adapts to gravity | Each crew member's bone and muscle; daily exercise against work hours |
| Planning with margins | The EVA route planner and Apollo's walkback limit |
| One failure drags others with it | *April 1970*: the oxygen tank bursts, the fuel cell dies and the CO₂ scrubbers fail all at once |
| A crew's autonomy | Solar conjunction on Mars: two weeks without warnings or help from Earth |
| Reading data and charting it | *Download data (CSV)*: the mission telemetry, ready for a spreadsheet |
| Area grows with the square of the diameter | A puncture twice the size loses four times as much air |
| With no air, nothing slows fragments | An impact's ejecta damages the base tens of metres away; Whipple shields |
| Procedures have a reason | Minigames: tightening in a star, purging valves in order, first aid step by step |
| Planning with reserves | Spare parts in the cargo instead of something else; improvising only simple fixes |

## Before you start

- **Introduce it in two minutes:** *Demo* in the main menu plays a short mission by itself with captions (building, exploring, sheltering, handling an emergency, the debrief). Project it at the start of the first session; any key hands control back.
- **Language:** Spanish, English, Portuguese and French, from the main menu or Settings. Useful for bilingual classes or newly arrived students: each can play the same mission as everyone else in their own language.
- **Tablets and Chromebooks:** the game picks the *Minimal* quality by itself and shows touch buttons for every action. If a device is slow, lower it by hand under Settings → Quality.

## Four 45-minute sessions

**Session 1 · First steps (Junior and Cadet).** The *First steps* mission (lunar training). The game guides every step: deploying arrays and a battery, going out on EVA, scanning, collecting a sample, driving the rover, dropping the hammer and the feather, sheltering from the storm. At the end, each student answers the four questions and downloads their report.

**Session 2 · The 1972 storm.** The *August 1972* scenario. Before playing, tell the class that between Apollo 16 and Apollo 17 there was a very intense solar storm and that nobody was on the Moon. Question to discuss before starting: what would you pack to be safe? After the mission, compare the reports: who stayed under the dose limit? What did they do differently?

**Session 3 · Mars is not the Moon (Cadet and Expert).** The *Jezero: Sol 0* or *September 2017* mission. In pairs: one plays, the other notes every time the power drops. Afterwards: why does a reactor justify its weight on Mars and perhaps not at the lunar pole? For Expert: repeat the hammer-and-feather drop on Mars and explain with numbers why the air hardly shows.

**Session 4 · Planning an outing (Cadet and Expert).** The *Connecting ridge* or *Malapert massif* mission. Before going out, each group draws a route to the expedition with *EVA route* and writes down what the planner predicts: distance, climb, suit on foot and by rover, battery. Then they do the EVA and compare with the suit they have left when they get back. To discuss: why did the Apollo missions never drive the rover further than they could walk back? What reserve would you keep, and why?

## More sessions

**Session 5 · A successful failure (Cadet and Expert).** The *April 1970* scenario. Before playing, tell the story of Apollo 13: at 55 hours and 55 minutes an oxygen tank burst, and three astronauts survived in a module built for two, improvising CO₂ filters out of tape and cardboard. In the game the same happens, but on the lunar surface. To discuss afterwards: what did you switch off first, and why? What would have happened without the backup canisters? Why does NASA call it "a successful failure"?

**Session 6 · The storm that silenced Opportunity (Expert).** The *June 2018* scenario. In 2018 a global dust storm left the Martian sky almost black for weeks, and the solar-powered Opportunity rover never woke up again. Have half the class take reactors and the other half solar arrays only, and compare. Then, with *Download data (CSV)*, chart sunlight and power over the mission in a spreadsheet: how many sols did the darkness last? When did production start to recover?

**A maths activity with the data.** Any mission works: the CSV has a row every few hours with power, oxygen, water, food, CO₂, dose, health, morale, science, temperature and sunlight. Ideas: work out the slope of the oxygen (kg per day) and compare it with 0.84 kg per person; find the lunar night in the sunlight column; add up the dose per day and compare it with a chest X-ray.

**Session 7 · Catastrophes and spare parts (Cadet and Expert).** The *March 2013* scenario (the impact) or *March 1973* (the quake). Before playing, each group decides how many crates of spare parts to take and what to leave on Earth to make room, and justifies it in writing. During the mission, they repair by hand whatever they can (the minigames) and note every piece of damage: what caused it, how far away, what it did and how many parts it took. Afterwards, compare: which group chose best? Which damage needed repairing first, and why (hint: whatever costs air)? For Expert: given that a 5 mm hole loses about 7 kg of air an hour, work out how much a 10 mm one would lose and explain why.

**Tasks and requests as teamwork.** In team mode, share the daily tasks and Mission Control's requests among the stations: life support cleans the filter, science analyses samples and sets out instruments, maintenance repairs. The daily task bar shows how many you did yourselves and how many the crew had to do.

## Discussion questions

1. If every kilo taken to Mars costs a fortune, which is better: carrying water or a machine that recycles it? After how many days does the answer change?
2. A solar array is light but depends on the Sun; a reactor is heavy but always works. Which would you choose for the lunar pole? And for Mars during a dust storm?
3. Why does the game not let you walk to the expedition? What really limits an astronaut on the surface?
4. The dose limit is not a wall: below it, receiving less is still worth it (ALARA). Why?
5. From Mars, a question to Earth takes half an hour to be answered. Which decisions should a Martian crew be ready to take alone?
6. During solar conjunction there is no contact with Earth for two weeks. What would you prepare beforehand? What rules would the crew agree on to decide without help?
7. On Apollo 13 not one thing failed but several at once, because they were connected. Find a failure in your base that could drag others with it. How would you prevent it?
8. Faced with a fire, fight it or vent the module? What do you gain and lose each way?
9. Why do some procedures have a fixed order (tightening in a star, purging valves, first aid)? What happens if you skip a step?
10. Every kilo of spare parts is a kilo less of something else. How would you decide how many to take to a base on Mars, which does not get cargo ships every few months?

## Catastrophes, tasks and minigames

- **Catastrophes.** Meteoroid impacts, quakes, fires, battery thermal runaways and computers crashed by solar storms leave concrete damage, visible in 3D and explained in each module's inspector (for example "Hull puncture · 5 mm · 2 kg of O₂/h escaping"). The Junior level has half as many, and training missions have no random catastrophes. If you set 0 emergencies in the editor, there will be none either.
- **Spare parts.** Repairs use parts; without them only simple fixes can be improvised. A good way into logistics: the ISS gets cargo several times a year, a base on Mars does not.
- **Minigames.** They last 10 to 30 seconds and the mission pauses meanwhile. Each shows the real fact it is based on. At Junior level they are simpler (fewer pieces, more margin). They work with mouse, finger or keyboard.
- **Daily tasks.** A short list each morning. If nobody does them, the crew does them when they fall due, at the cost of maintenance hours: nobody loses a mission for skipping them, but it shows.
- **Mission Control requests.** Jobs with a deadline and a science reward (instruments, cores, photos, inspections). A forgotten inspection ends in a leak: a good opening to talk about preventive maintenance.
- **Upgrades.** At each science milestone you choose one of three real technologies: ask the group to explain their choice before they click.

## The campaign

In the menu, **Campaign** puts nine missions in order of difficulty: each chapter teaches one idea (deploying power, polar shadows, the 1972 storm, two-week nights away from the pole, a base on real terrain, the nuclear reactor, Mars, its atmosphere during a solar storm, living off what the base produces) and opens when the previous one is completed. Medals (bronze for completing, silver from 65 points, gold from 80) are saved on each computer. It is a good sequence for independent work or homework; every mission is still open in *New mission* if you prefer to choose.

## The mission editor

In *New mission → Mission editor* you can design your own mission: destination, length, crew, maximum cargo, science goal, dose limit, how many solar and dust storms (and how strong), emergencies, radio delay, return fuel, resupplies, whether it is played on one of the five real terrains and, on Mars, whether there is a solar conjunction (missions of 20 sols or more). All of it becomes a **15-character code** you copy onto the board; each student types it into *Your teacher's code* and plays exactly the same mission, with the same events in the same order. *Variant* reshuffles when events happen without changing anything else: useful so that two groups cannot copy each other. Ideas:

- **Compare one variable**: two codes that differ in a single thing (for example a budget that does or does not allow recycling, or 3 versus 20 minutes of radio delay), each half of the class plays one.
- **A tailored challenge**: for Junior, a short mission with no emergencies; for Expert, severe storms and little cargo.
- **A practical test**: a code with an ambitious science goal and a strict dose limit; the downloaded report records the result.

## Team mode

On the cargo manifest, **Team play** lets 2 to 5 students share one computer. Each looks after one or two stations for the whole mission (power, life support, health and radiation, science, maintenance) and the controls rotate in shifts of 12 h, one day or three mission days. At the end of each shift the game stops and shows which goals each station met, who takes the controls and the goals for the next shift. Warnings carry the name of whoever should react. It works well with one-day shifts and a simple rule: whoever is at the controls decides, everyone else watches their station and advises out loud. The debrief includes a table with each student's shifts and goals, useful for assessing group work.

## The daily challenge

In the mission list, the **daily challenge** is a short mission generated from the date. The same code gives everyone the same mission: project the code on the board, have each student (or pair) play it and compare scores and reports. To replay a challenge on another day, type its code. Scores are kept on each computer; if you use the page published on claude.ai, you can also gather them with *Class results* (see below).

## The report

At the end of every mission, **Download report for the teacher** produces an HTML file with the outcome, the score by area, the mission's lessons and the answers to the questions (with the right one and the explanation). It opens in any browser and prints on one or two pages. The student's name is optional and never leaves the computer.

Next to it, **Download data (CSV)** saves the mission telemetry to work on in a spreadsheet (it is also in the pause menu). In Spanish, Portuguese and French it uses semicolons and decimal commas, so Excel, LibreOffice or Google Sheets open it correctly first time.

## Class results

If you publish the game as a claude.ai page (the `dist-web/helios-outpost.html` build, which has a shared database), **Class results** appears:

1. Share the page with your students with permission to **take part** (*Contributor*). With view-only access they can play but not send.
2. After a mission, each student presses **Send to the class**. Their best score for that mission is stored; if they try again and do worse, the best one is kept.
3. Whoever published the page (or anyone with edit access) opens **Class results** from the main menu, picks the mission and sees everyone's table: name, result, score, peak dose, science and when it was sent. The table updates by itself and downloads as CSV.

Each student can only see and change their own row. Only numbers and the mission id are sent, never text a student typed, and only when the button is pressed. The name the teacher sees is the one on each student's account. In the desktop program and in the `helios-outpost.html` file there is no database: the game sends nothing anywhere.

## Rigour and limits

Human consumption, gravity, radiation, sunlight, day length and radio delays are real figures; the simplifications and their reasons are in [`SCIENCE.en.md`](SCIENCE.en.md). It is worth saying in class: a simulator helps to understand relationships (more shielding, less dose; less gravity, less grip), it does not replace the data of a real mission.
